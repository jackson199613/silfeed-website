How to Prevent Premix Caking in Tropical Feed Mills Without Losing Enzyme Activity

Separating the two failure modes behind caking and enzyme loss in tropical feed production, and the carrier specifications that address each.

Premix caking in high-humidity storage and enzyme loss during 95 C conditioning are two separate failure modes. Caking is driven by capillary condensation between particles; enzyme loss is driven by steam penetration into the carrier pore structure. A carrier needs a hydrophobic surface for the first problem and an intact mesoporous shield for the second.

Humidity and heat attack a carrier in two different ways

Tropical feed production exposes a premix to two stresses that most carrier specifications treat as one. High ambient humidity acts on the outside of the particle, while steam conditioning acts on the inside of the pore network. A carrier that solves one does not automatically solve the other.

Capillary condensation is the mechanism behind the first stress. Water vapour condenses in the narrow gaps between adjacent particles well below the dew point of the bulk air, because the curved meniscus in a narrow gap lowers the local vapour pressure required for condensation. Those liquid bridges then carry dissolved salts, and as the premix sits in storage the salts recrystallise and convert a reversible bridge into a solid neck. The result is the hard cake that blocks pneumatic lines and bridges in hoppers.

Surface energy governs whether those bridges form at all. The Silfeed HC Series carries a 110° hydrophobic barrier, meaning the water contact angle on the treated surface exceeds 90° and the surface is non-wetting. A non-wetting surface inverts the sign of the meniscus curvature, so condensation in the inter-particle gap requires a higher vapour pressure rather than a lower one. The Laplace pressure threshold for bridge formation rises accordingly, and this threshold is verified as a pass criterion on every batch.

Steam conditioning is a separate problem with a separate solution. During 95 C conditioning, steam penetrates the carrier and reaches the bio-active payload held inside the pore structure. Protection here depends on the geometry of the pore network rather than the surface chemistry. The Hexagonal Mesoporous Shield (HMS) architecture used in the HC Series raises the tortuosity factor of the diffusion path, so steam takes longer to reach the payload than the residence time of the conditioner. HMS pore integrity at 95 C is verified at at least 98% intact, and the shield stays active to a peak of 105 C.

A four-step selection framework

Carrier selection becomes deterministic once the two stresses above are separated. Work through the steps in order, because a later step can override an earlier one.

Step 1 — Fix the liquid load first

Liquid load sets the floor on oil absorption. A target load below roughly 35% of carrier weight is served by HC-100 at an oil absorption of at least 280 g/100 g. Loads approaching 65%, which is the level demonstrated in production with essential oils, require HC-200 at at least 320 g/100 g. Selecting a carrier below the load requirement produces a powder that appears free-flowing at packing and releases oil in storage.

Step 2 — Check the peak process temperature

Peak temperature overrides the load decision when a bio-active is present. Any process reaching 95 C steam conditioning with enzymes or probiotics in the formulation moves the selection to HC-Bio, whose shield remains active to 105 C. Thermal exposure is the one criterion that cannot be compensated by increasing carrier dosage.

Step 3 — Match particle size to the mixing system

Particle size governs distribution in the mixer rather than capacity. HC-100 at D50 10–15 µm suits standard premix ribbon and paddle mixers. HC-200 at D50 5–8 µm gives the higher specific surface needed for high-load adsorption, and benefits from a longer dry-mix stage before liquid addition.

Step 4 — Verify against storage humidity, not ambient humidity

Storage conditions decide the caking outcome, and warehouse humidity in tropical markets frequently exceeds the figure quoted for the production floor. Specify against the 95% relative humidity condition under which the HC Series flow data was generated, rather than against an annual average.

Frequently asked questions

What causes premix caking at high relative humidity?

Premix caking in high-humidity storage is driven by capillary condensation. Water vapour condenses in the gaps between particles and forms liquid bridges, which then harden into solid necks as the premix sits in storage. A carrier with a hydrophobic surface raises the vapour pressure required for that condensation to start, which is why contact angle matters more than bulk moisture content.

How much liquid can a silica carrier hold before flow is lost?

Loading capacity tracks oil absorption. Silfeed HC-100 is specified at an oil absorption of at least 280 g/100 g, and HC-200 at least 320 g/100 g, measured by the ISO 787-5 method. In production, HC-200 has been used to convert 65% liquid essential oils into a free-flowing stable powder matrix.

Do enzymes survive 95 C steam conditioning on a silica carrier?

Enzyme survival depends on whether the carrier pore structure stays closed during conditioning. Silfeed HC Series retains at least 88% enzyme activity through 95 C steam conditioning, with HMS pore integrity verified at 95 C to be at least 98% intact. The shield remains active to a peak of 105 C.
